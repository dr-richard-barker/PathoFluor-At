Can fluorescence hyperspectral imaging see a seedling respond to bacteria?

In 2016 we imaged Arabidopsis seedlings by fluorescence hyperspectral imaging before and after treatment with a mock solution, the flagellin epitope flg22 or Pseudomonas syringae. Five genotypes were imaged: Col-0, two fls2 flagellin-receptor mutants and two cml24 Ca2+-sensor mutants. Our reanalysis finds a pathogen signature from 1 hour after inoculation. It also finds that the flg22 signal in this experiment does not depend on FLS2, so it is not perception.

The experiment

Seedlings on square agar plates were imaged in the Gilroy Lab (UW-Madison Botany) with a MacroPhor fluorescence hyperspectral line-scan imager (Middleton Spectral Vision), using 488 nm excitation and recording 324 bands from 400 to 809 nm. Each plate held five genotype lanes and was imaged as three row-scans. Four plates per treatment were imaged before treatment and at 1, 2, 3, 24 and 48 h. An independent November 2016 replicate used 405 nm excitation with Col-0 and both fls2 lines. Facts still to be confirmed (allele stock records, growth light and temperature) are listed openly in FACTS_PENDING.md.

The decisive control is genetic. An flg22 signature that reflects perception must disappear in fls2 mutants, which lack the flagellin receptor. The pathogen is still perceived through other bacterial patterns, so its signature need not.

Every seedling in the raw cubes

{{nSeedA}} seedlings segmented from the July cubes reproduce the early signature without the 2016 annotations. 685 nm emission then falls (by {{pathSeedCpsFortyEight}} counts s−1 at 48 h) and seedlings shrink. Curve resolution finds no separate broad autofluorescence component. Bacteria-only scans do not fluoresce, so the signal is the plant's. In the November replicate, a deteriorating Control plate showed the largest short-wavelength rise, so that emission marks damage, not infection specifically. No CML24 effect replicated.

From plate to practice: early detection outside the lab

Chlorophyll fluorescence imaging is an established way to see how pathogens affect photosynthesis (Rolfe & Scholes 2010). Optical sensors of several kinds are being developed to detect disease early, from single leaves to whole fields (Mahlein 2016). What does this experiment add? We asked two practical questions, fixed in advance and committed before the analysis was run.

1. If a scanner had been watching these plates, what would it have flagged?

We replayed the experiment as a monitoring run. At each hour, every plate was scored by a model that had never seen it, trained on how each plate's Col-0 emission spectra had changed since its own pre-treatment scan. A plate is flagged when its probability of infection exceeds 0.5. Press play to watch the run unfold.

Cells: the plate's mean held-out probability of infection. ▲ with an outline = flagged. flg22 plates were never used in training; they test whether the alarm also fires on a non-bacterial challenge. Control-4 and flg22-2 are excluded because their hour-0 scans were partly upright rescans. The run stops at 24 h because Pathogen 48 h has no hand-drawn spectra. The flg22 plates were flagged too, at {{alarmFlgEarlyHits}} of {{alarmFlgEarlyCells}} plate-hours in the first 3 h, but {{alarmFlgLateHits}} of {{nAlarmFlgPlates}} at 24 h, when {{alarmPathLateHits}} of {{nAlarmPathPlates}} Pathogen plates were still flagged. So a single flag means "this plant has been challenged"; a flag that persists is what pointed to infection. With three or four plates per group this is a demonstration, not a validated detector.

This fits earlier work in which P. syringae changed photosynthesis and chlorophyll fluorescence before symptoms (Bonfig et al. 2006).

3. Could a cheap filter camera do it?

No. Reduced to two to four {{bandWidthNm}} nm filter bands, the best plate AUC for Pathogen vs Control was {{bandFiltAucMax}} (smallest permutation p = {{bandFiltPMin}}); the classic F685/F740 ratio reached at most {{bandTwoAucMax}}. The full 2 nm spectrum reached {{bandFullAucMin}}–{{bandFullAucMax}}. The signal is a broadening of the red peak by {{pathFwhmOnenm}}–{{pathFwhmTwonm}} nm, which wide filters average away. A practical sensor needs spectrometer-class resolution across 650–760 nm.

4. What a working system would need, and why

  1. Scan the same plants from the startCompare every plant with its own baseline: plates here differed before treatment (AUC {{aucPlaceboFlg}} at hour 0).
  2. Fix and record exposureThe 2016 summaries' "drop" after treatment was a camera setting: {{rawDropPct}}% raw, {{cpsDiffPct}}% once corrected.
  3. Measure the dark level in every scan{{nOffsetRoi}} exported spectra kept a ~{{offsetMedian}}-count offset that looked like autofluorescence.
  4. Never saturateHour-0 scans saturated up to {{satHzeroMaxPct}}% of a cube's seedling pixels.
  5. Resolve the red peak at ~2 nmFilter bands lost the signal (best AUC {{bandFiltAucMax}}).
  6. Find each plant automaticallySegmenting {{nSeedA}} seedlings from the raw cubes reproduced the signature without hand annotation.
  7. Flag on change, confirm on persistenceflg22 plates were flagged at 1–3 h but not at 24 h; Pathogen plates stayed flagged.
  8. Confirm before actingThe signature is not pathogen-specific: the flg22 signal did not need FLS2, and short-wavelength emission also rose on a dying Control plate.

5. Where it could be used first

Controlled-environment farmsgreenhouses, vertical farms, growth rooms

Closest to this experiment. Lights can be switched off for a scan, geometry is fixed, and the same plants can be rescanned on a schedule from a gantry or conveyor, so the baseline and exposure rules above can be met.

Still missing: older plants and canopies instead of seedlings on agar; other crops, pathogens and strains; many more than four plates per group.

Space crop systemsVeggie, Advanced Plant Habitat, future lunar greenhouses

Disease has already happened there. Zinnias grown in Veggie on the ISS in late 2015 developed a Fusarium oxysporum infection linked to reduced airflow and water build-up (Schuerger et al. 2021). Habitats are enclosed and controlled like the farms above, and crops are few and slow to replace.

Still missing: a compact flight-qualified imager, and tests with fungal pathogens and flight crops.

Open fieldstractors, drones, satellites

Hardest. Sunlight itself excites chlorophyll fluorescence, measured as solar-induced fluorescence from towers, aircraft and satellites (Mohammed et al. 2019), but at canopy scale, far coarser than the seedling spectra here. An actively excited scanner like this one would have to work at night, under a hood, or with excitation it can separate from daylight.

Still missing: all of the above, plus distance, canopy structure, mixed pixels and weather.

What a flag does not tell you

  • Which pathogen, or that it is a pathogen at all: the flg22 treatment and plain damage also shifted the spectrum.
  • That the plant perceived flagellin: the flg22 signal here did not depend on FLS2.
  • Anything beyond this setting: four plates per group, one bacterial strain, seedlings on agar, 488 nm excitation.

The data in 3D and 4D

A hyperspectral image is natively three-dimensional: two dimensions of position and one of wavelength. These plates were scanned six times, so time is a fourth. Below, one row of seedlings is shown as a cloud of (position, wavelength) points you can turn, slice and step through time. The plates' feature changes are drawn as paths through a three-dimensional space. We did not draw spectrum-versus-time as a 3D surface: its peaks hide its own valleys, so that view stays as the 2D explorer and Fig. 2.
